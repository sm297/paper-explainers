When an ion’s charge flickers, heat moves

In a dense plasma, bound electrons are knocked off and recaptured over and over. Each time, an ion’s charge changes, and its neighbours feel a different push. In this paper we show that this restless flicker is a route for energy to flow between hot electrons and cold ions, and that it can be as fast as ordinary collisions.

Two temperatures that won’t stay apart

Many ways of making matter extremely hot and dense heat only one species. An x-ray free-electron laser or an optical laser dumps its energy into electrons, leaving the ions comparatively cold. A shock wave does the opposite: it heats ions and barely touches the electrons. For a while the plasma has two temperatures, \(T_e\) and \(T_i\), and how quickly they meet controls what experiments measure, how astrophysical shocks glow and whether fusion fuel ignites.

The textbook picture, from Landau and Spitzer, is binary collisions: a light, fast electron bounces off a heavy, slow ion and hands over a sliver of energy each time. Modern versions describe the same physics as electron and ion density fluctuations rubbing against each other. Yet measured equilibration times in apparently similar conditions range from under a picosecond to nanoseconds. Something is missing.

Almost every calculation treats the ionization state through an average: a mean charge \(\bar Z\), or at best a steady population of charge states. Our starting point is that an individual ion never sits at the average. Its charge jumps up on collisional ionization and down on recombination, even when the plasma as a whole is in steady state.

The cycle that pumps energy

Bound electrons screen an ion from its neighbours, so changing an ion’s charge changes how hard it pushes on the ions around it. This works both ways. Raising the charge of an ion that has another positive ion close by costs extra Coulomb energy, so the energy needed to ionize it depends on where its neighbouring ions are. That shared link lets energy flow in either direction through the same four-stage cycle. The animation above shows the forward version.

One cycle of ionization and recombination, run in each direction
StageForward: electrons → ionsReverse: ions → electrons
1. Ionization Neighbouring ion is close. Ionizing costs extra Coulomb energy, so the electron pays a lot. Neighbouring ion is far. Ionizing costs little extra, so the electron pays a little.
2. Neighbouring ion moves Stronger repulsion pushes the neighbouring ion out. It gains kinetic energy. Neighbouring ion moves in against the stronger repulsion. It loses kinetic energy.
3. Recombination Neighbouring ion is far. The electron gets back a little. Neighbouring ion is close. The electron gets back a lot.
4. Reset Repulsion is weak again. The neighbouring ion drifts back in, keeping its extra energy. Repulsion is weak again. The neighbouring ion drifts back out, without the energy it lost.
Net Electrons lose energy, ions gain it. Ions lose energy, electrons gain it.

What decides the direction. In both columns the extra Coulomb energy is the shift in ionization energy caused by the neighbouring ion. Hot electrons make the forward ordering more common, by driving charge changes that push the ions. Hot ions make the reverse ordering more common, because their motion shifts the ionization energy and so biases when ionization and recombination happen. When \(T_e = T_i\) the two are equally likely and cancel.

Ultracold-plasma physicists know a cousin of this effect: the burst of heating when atoms are suddenly ionized, called disorder-induced heating. Here it happens continuously and in both directions.

A charge that random-walks

To compute anything, you need the statistics of the flicker. We model each ion’s charge as a random walk: it steps up by one with the collisional ionization rate and down by one with the recombination rate. Hydrogen is the cleanest case, with only two states, neutral and bare proton. If the mean time to ionize is \(\tau_\alpha\) and the mean time to recombine is \(\tau_\beta\), the charge fluctuations forget themselves exponentially:

\[\langle \delta Z(t)\,\delta Z(0)\rangle=\frac{\tau_\alpha\tau_\beta}{(\tau_\alpha+\tau_\beta)^2}\,\exp\!\left[-t\left(\frac{1}{\tau_\alpha}+\frac{1}{\tau_\beta}\right)\right]\]

The figure simulates that random walk directly. Drag the sliders and check the simulated correlation against the formula.

For heavier atoms there are more rungs on the ladder, so the correlation becomes a sum of exponentials, one per relaxation mode of the walk. Each mode has an amplitude \(A_i\) and a lifetime \(\tau_i\), and its contribution to the spectrum is a Lorentzian:

\[\langle \delta Z(\omega)\,\delta Z(-\omega)\rangle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_i \frac{A_i\,\tau_i}{1+\omega^2\tau_i^2}\]

Why an indirect route can win

Electrons and ions exchange energy only at frequencies where both can move. Electrons oscillate near the electron plasma frequency; ions near the far lower ion plasma frequency. For hydrogen the two differ by \(\sqrt{m_p/m_e}\approx 43\), and the electron and ion spectra barely overlap. That mismatch is why direct electron–ion coupling is so slow.

Charge fluctuations are different. Their spectrum is set by ionization and recombination rates, which in dense plasmas often fall right where the ions respond. Even though charge fluctuations are much weaker than electron density fluctuations, the better frequency match can make up for it. This is the key physical insight behind the second figure in our paper.

The exchange rate, and which way it runs

We derive the rate using linear response theory, by the same steps used for density-fluctuation theories of direct collisions. A change \(\delta Z\) in an ion’s charge changes the Coulomb potential around it; the neighbouring ions respond through their response function \(\chi_{ii}\); and the charge fluctuations themselves obey a fluctuation–dissipation theorem with a charge response function \(\chi_{ZZ}\). That gives the forward rate. For the reverse process, the thermal motion of the ions shifts the ionization energy by an amount \(\delta I\), the same extra Coulomb energy as in the table above, and this biases when ionization and recombination happen. Adding the two gives the net rate at which ions gain energy:

\[\frac{dE_i}{dt}=n_i\!\int\!\frac{d\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^3}\!\int\!\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\hbar\omega\left(\frac{4\pi e^2}{k^2}\right)^{\!2}\operatorname{Im}\chi_{ii}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\,\operatorname{Im}\chi_{ZZ}(\omega)\left[\coth\frac{\hbar\omega}{2T_e}-\coth\frac{\hbar\omega}{2T_i}\right]\]

This has the same form as the familiar formula for direct collisions, with the electron spectrum swapped for the charge-state spectrum. The bracket at the end decides the direction of flow. At the frequencies that matter for ions, \(\hbar\omega\) is tiny compared with either temperature and the bracket reduces to something proportional to \(T_e - T_i\). It vanishes in equilibrium and flips sign when the ions are hotter.

For liquid-density hydrogen with 1 eV ions, we find that this channel is comparable to the Landau–Spitzer rate for direct collisions as long as the electron temperature stays below about the ionization energy. Hotter than that, the hydrogen is almost fully ionized, there is little left to flicker, and the channel fades.

Carbon: more shells, richer behaviour

With six electrons, carbon’s random walk has seven states and no tidy formula, so we simulate it by Monte Carlo and fit the correlation function with a few exponentials. At any given temperature only a handful of charge states are populated, so one or two modes dominate. They can be traced to three families of fluctuations: the 2p electrons (charge 0–2), the 2s electrons (2–4) and the 1s electrons (4–6).

That mirrors our carbon result in the paper: the peak transfer rate comes at lower temperature, driven by the 2s shell, and the window where the mechanism matters is limited.

In solid-density carbon heated by an XFEL, measured equilibration was much faster than older theories predicted. We find the charge-fluctuation rate there is comparable to direct electron–ion collisions, a candidate for part of the gap.

Metals and shocks: when the flicker switches off

Now drag the pressure-ionization slider to the right. Squeeze atoms hard enough and their outer electrons are no longer bound at all; they join the conduction band. That is the normal state of a metal. A shell that cannot hold electrons cannot flicker, so whole families of modes vanish and the coupling collapses.

In the paper we show this for solid-density aluminium with hot ions and cooler electrons. The pressure-ionized M shell is gone, and the cool electrons lack the energy to ionize the L shell, so the charge-fluctuation channel is strongly suppressed. That offers a tidy explanation for a puzzle: shock-heated metals have shown slow equilibration, while XFEL-heated carbon equilibrated fast. Different materials and heating routes switch this channel on or off.

One more consequence we point out in the paper: if intense radiation rather than collisions drives the ionization, the same mechanism would couple the radiation field directly to the ions, bypassing the electrons.

What to take away

Partially ionized dense plasmas have a third player in temperature equilibration: the fluctuating charge of individual ions. It acts as a go-between that is well tuned to ion motion, so despite being an indirect process it can rival direct collisions. Its strength depends sharply on temperature, atomic shell structure and pressure ionization, and that sensitivity is exactly the kind of thing that could make measured equilibration times scatter from one experiment to the next.

Our calculations treat the ion response in the random phase approximation and the fluctuations with a single-step random walk, so the numbers are estimates rather than final predictions. The main contribution of the paper is the mechanism and the framework for computing it.
