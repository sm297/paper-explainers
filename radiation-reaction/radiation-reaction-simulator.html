Radiation reaction, three ways

An electron beam crashes head-on into an intense laser pulse, wiggles, radiates gamma rays, and slows down. How much it slows depends on whether you describe the radiation classically or quantum mechanically. Move the sliders to see the three models used in Los et al., Nature Communications (2026) disagree.

Things to try

  • Pick Nearly classical. All three curves sit on top of each other: at low η, quantum corrections are tiny.
  • Pick Deep quantum. Classical radiation reaction overshoots badly, and the stochastic model spreads the beam out while the continuous ones squeeze it.
  • Pick Misaligned. Now every model broadens the beam, because electrons meet different field strengths. This is why the experiment couldn’t prove the broadening came from quantum randomness.

How the three models differ

  • Classical (Landau–Lifshitz): smooth, continuous energy loss, with no cap on photon energy.
  • Quantum-continuous: still smooth, but the loss rate is reduced by the Gaunt factor g(η), matching the average quantum loss.
  • Quantum-stochastic: electrons emit discrete photons at random times, each taking a random share of their energy.

What this toy leaves out

Simplifications
  • Plane-wave, linearly polarised, Gaussian pulse at 0.8 μm, met exactly head-on at focus; no focusing geometry or timing offset.
  • Emission uses the locally constant field approximation, with the field strength resolved through each laser cycle.
  • Transverse misalignment is modelled by giving each electron a reduced peak field, a₀·exp(−r²/w₀²).
  • No pair production, no electron deflection, no detector response. This is not the authors’ simulation code, and it won’t reproduce their fitted numbers.
Toy model built to accompany E. E. Los et al., “Observation of quantum effects on radiation reaction in strong fields”, Nat. Commun. 17, 1157 (2026). Electron spectra use 1,500 simulated electrons; the stochastic curve is Monte Carlo, so it has some noise.
