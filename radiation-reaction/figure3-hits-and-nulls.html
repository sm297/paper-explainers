Hits, nulls and five sigma

The experiment never measured an electron beam both before and after the collision. Instead it fired hundreds of shots, sorted them into hits (the laser met the beam) and nulls (it missed, or was switched off), and asked whether hits came out with less energy. This widget runs a pretend campaign so you can see how beam and alignment jitter make that question harder, the idea behind Figure 3 of Los et al. (2026).

Sorting shots into hits and nulls

Every shot makes some gamma rays, even without a collision, and that background grows with beam charge and energy. A hit is a shot whose gamma signal sits well above the background line. Laser-off shots always count as nulls.

Things to try

  • Pick A handful of shots, roughly what earlier all-optical experiments had. Even with the same physics, the difference rarely clears 3σ.
  • From Like the experiment, press Rerun a few times. The significance jumps around: one campaign is one draw.
  • Turn energy jitter up. ⟨E⟩ suffers most, because a beam that simply came out of the accelerator cooler looks like energy loss. P₇₀ holds up better.
  • Raise the hit threshold. Hits get fewer but cleaner, so each one shows more energy loss.

Reading the real Figure 3

  • Panels a and b are the “Every shot” view: hits shifted to lower ⟨E⟩ and lower P₇₀.
  • Panels c and d are the “Bootstrapped means” view: the paper found the means separated by about 3σ for ⟨E⟩ and 5σ for P₇₀.
  • Panels e and f are the trend plot. Nulls drift slightly up with gamma yield, while hits fall, so a different process is at work in hits.

What this toy leaves out

Simplifications
  • Each shot has 500 electrons drawn from a peak plus a broad low-energy tail; electrons below 300 MeV are invisible, as on the real spectrometer.
  • Energy loss uses the quantum-continuous model with a fitted Gaunt factor, for a 45 fs, 0.8 μm Gaussian pulse with a 2.5 μm spot. The paper’s Figure 3 doesn’t depend on which model is right.
  • Gamma background is log-normal noise on a fixed scaling; 30% of shots have the laser off.
  • Significance uses a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and a bootstrap of the means, as in the paper. This isn’t the authors’ data or code, so the numbers are illustrative.
