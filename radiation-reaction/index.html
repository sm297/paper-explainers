An interactive guide to a 2026 Nature Communications paper

When light pushes back

Shake an electron hard enough and it glows, and every bit of light it gives off pushes it back a little. Physicists call this push radiation reaction. Under ordinary conditions it’s far too small to notice. But in the field of the world’s most intense lasers, an electron can lose a large share of its energy in a few femtoseconds, and how much it loses depends on whether you treat the light classically or as quantum photons.

In January 2026, a team led from Imperial College London reported the clearest test yet. At the Gemini laser in the UK, they fired electron beams moving at nearly the speed of light head-on into a laser pulse more than 600 times. The electrons that collided came out with measurably less energy, a result significant beyond the 5σ level physicists require to call something an observation. When they compared models, the quantum descriptions explained the data better than the classical one.

These pages rebuild the paper’s main ideas as small simulations you can play with. They’re toy models: simplified, but built on the same physics. Each page explains what it leaves out.
