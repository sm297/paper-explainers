Fitting one shot, three ways

For each bright shot, the experiment knew the electron spectrum after the collision and the gamma rays it produced, but not exactly how strong the laser was where the beam crossed it. Figure 4 of Los et al. (2026) shows each model being tuned to fit one such shot. Here you get a simulated shot and can try the fit yourself, then let Bayesian inference do it properly.

Misfit is χ² per data point: around 1 means the model matches within the error bars; much bigger means it doesn’t. Try to get both close to 1 with each model.

Electron spectrum

Grey is the beam before the collision, known only from a prediction; points are what was measured after it. Each model has to turn the grey curve into the points.

Gamma rays in the detector

Energy left in each 5 mm slab of a caesium-iodide stack. Higher-energy photons penetrate deeper, so the shape of this curve encodes the photon spectrum.

What Bayesian inference concludes

Each model is scored over every combination of laser strength and spread, not just its best one. The curves show how plausible each laser strength is under each model.

Building model tables…

Things to try

  • Fit the electron spectrum with Classical, then switch to a quantum model without moving the sliders. The quantum fit loses too little energy: classical needs a weaker laser to match the same shift, just as in the paper.
  • Check the gamma-ray misfit at each model’s best fit. The electrons can be matched by every model; the photons are what separate them.
  • Fit Quantum-continuous to a stochastic shot. It needs a larger spread σ to fake the extra broadening, the degeneracy that kept the paper from telling the two quantum models apart.
  • Turn the detector noise up. The Bayes factors shrink toward 1: noisier photons can’t break the tie.

Reading the real Figure 4

  • Panel a is the electron spectrum: the orange band is the neural network’s guess at the beam before collision, red is measured, and coloured lines are each model’s fit.
  • Panel b is energy deposited along the photon detector.
  • The caption lists each model’s inferred ⟨ã₀⟩: about 6.2 for classical and 6.7–6.8 for the quantum models, the same pattern you’ll see here.
  • One shot gives only weak evidence. Figure 5 multiplies the evidence from ten shots.

What this toy leaves out

Simplifications
  • The pre-collision spectrum is treated as known. In the paper it was a neural-network prediction with its own uncertainty, which is part of why their fits were looser.
  • Two parameters stand in for the paper’s three (a₀, beam duration and collision offset): the average field electrons felt and its spread.
  • The stochastic model is approximated by its mean and variance (a Fokker–Planck treatment) rather than full Monte Carlo, and shares the quantum-continuous photon spectrum.
  • Photon deposition uses a textbook shower profile in CsI instead of a Geant4 simulation. Inference is a brute-force grid with flat priors, not MCMC with LOO-PSIS.
Toy model built to accompany E. E. Los et al., “Observation of quantum effects on radiation reaction in strong fields”, Nat. Commun. 17, 1157 (2026). The simulated shot and all numbers here are illustrative, not the paper’s data.
