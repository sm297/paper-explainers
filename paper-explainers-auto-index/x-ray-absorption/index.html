An X-ray snapshot of matter, taken in a few femtoseconds

Our two experiments at the Gemini laser show that the X-rays shaken out of electrons in a laser-driven plasma wave are bright and smooth enough to record a full absorption spectrum in a single shot.

The problem: hot, dense, and gone in a flash

Matter driven to extreme temperatures and pressures, the stuff inside planets or fusion capsules, changes within femtoseconds to picoseconds, and each sample is usually destroyed by the shot that heats it. To study it you want a probe that is ultrashort, penetrating, and complete in one exposure.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy is ideal in principle. Shine broadband X-rays through a sample and look at the absorption around an inner-shell edge: the edge itself reports on the electrons, and the ripples after it report on where the neighbouring ions sit. The catch is the source. Synchrotrons are broadband but their pulses last ~100 ps. X-ray free-electron lasers are femtosecond but nearly monochromatic, so a spectrum needs many shots. Big-laser backlighters need hundreds of joules and last over 100 ps.

The source: electrons wiggling in a plasma bubble

A ~45 fs laser pulse focused into gas blows the electrons out of its path, leaving a positively charged bubble behind it. Electrons injected at the back of the bubble are accelerated to hundreds of MeV or more in millimetres, and the bubble’s fields make them oscillate sideways as they go. Those betatron oscillations radiate a forward cone of X-rays lasting about as long as the electron bunch, around 10 fs.

The spectrum looks like synchrotron radiation and is set by a single number, the critical energy Ecrit. The key point for spectroscopy is that it is smooth: across the narrow window a crystal spectrometer sees, there are no sharp features to confuse with the sample’s own.

What an absorption spectrum tells you

Just below an element’s K-edge the sample is fairly transparent. At the edge, photons gain enough energy to eject a 1s electron and absorption jumps. Two regions of the result carry different physics.

XANES, the first few tens of eV, is about electrons. The edge sits where empty states begin, so its slope traces the Fermi distribution: heat the electrons and the edge foot shifts and softens. Small pre-edge bumps show transitions into bound states such as titanium’s 3d shell.

EXAFS, from roughly 30 eV to hundreds of eV above the edge, is about ions. The ejected photoelectron scatters off neighbouring atoms and interferes with itself, producing ripples whose frequencies encode the distances to each shell of neighbours. Fourier-transforming them gives something close to a radial distribution function. Hot ions jiggle, which washes the ripples out.

The hard part: enough signal in one shot

Photon counting is a Poisson process, so the best possible signal-to-noise ratio in a spectral bin is √N. In 2019 our brightest shots delivered about 1.2 million photons per eV, which should have allowed roughly 1100:1. The measured value was 300:1, because the GeV electron beam was hitting the chamber and spraying the detector with secondary radiation. That was still enough for clean XANES on titanium, but EXAFS needs something nearer 1000:1.

For the 2024 experiment we attacked this directly. FLUKA simulations guided our redesign: sweep electrons the opposite way to the X-rays, into a lead-shielded dump, and box the CCD in its own shielding with a shielded tunnel toward the crystal. We also switched to a cylindrically curved crystal, which focused the X-rays to a line, concentrating signal. The result was close to the Poisson limit: about 500 photons per spectral pixel with a standard deviation of about 23.

What each experiment showed

In 2019, a 10 µm titanium foil’s edge, its two pre-edge peaks and the near-edge structure matched synchrotron data in a single shot. Fitting consecutive shots, the edge position wandered by only 0.17 eV (standard error), enough to resolve an electron temperature change of about 0.4 eV in one shot. The narrow ≈80 eV window meant the ionic structure was out of reach; averaging 11 shots halved the post-edge error, hinting at what better shielding could do.

In 2024, a 4 µm copper foil was measured over 250 eV per shot, with the EXAFS ripples clearly visible. After Fourier transforming, the single-shot nearest-neighbour peak matched the Diamond Light Source reference to within 1.5%, and the next three shells within 5%. Averaging 10 shots tightened shells two to four to 2%, 3% and 5%. We also measured a real copper hydrogenation catalyst from Johnson Matthey in 30 shots, correctly identifying the copper as mostly Cu²⁺.

Where it goes next

In the 2024 paper we project how the platform scales. Using published betatron scalings, critical energy rises roughly linearly with laser power and peak brightness as about P1.6. Petawatt lasers firing at 1 Hz, twenty times Gemini’s rate, are becoming common, and density-tailored targets have shown roughly tenfold brighter emission on their own.

We estimate ×26 brightness, Ecrit ≈ 150 keV and about ×9 flux near 9 keV at 1 PW. Most of the brightness gain moves to harder photons, which opens edges above 30 keV that synchrotrons struggle to reach.

Beyond high-energy-density physics, we see applications in femtochemistry, photocatalysts, battery and spintronic materials, and radiological samples, all places where a broadband, femtosecond, lab-sized X-ray probe that is naturally synchronised to a pump laser could add something synchrotrons and XFELs cannot.
